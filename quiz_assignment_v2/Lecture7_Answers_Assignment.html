Q1. Connecting a \(224\times224\times3\) image to 1,000 dense hidden units needs about how many first-layer weights?

  • A. 150 thousand
  • B. 1.5 million
  • C. 150 million
  • D. 15 billion
Answer C. \(150{,}528\times1{,}000\approx1.5\times10^8\): far too many for typical defence datasets.

Q2. Which pair of ideas lets a CNN use far fewer parameters than a dense network on images?

  • A. Dropout and batch normalisation
  • B. Local connectivity and weight sharing
  • C. Softmax and cross-entropy
  • D. Large learning rate and momentum
Answer B. Each unit looks at a small patch, and the same kernel is reused at every position.

Q3. An edge-detecting kernel applied to a patch of uniform brightness gives:

  • A. 0
  • B. The maximum response
  • C. A negative response
  • D. The patch's mean brightness
Answer A. Its positive and negative weights sum to zero, so it responds only where brightness changes.

Q4. A \(3\times3\) kernel slides with stride 1 and no padding over a \(6\times6\) image. The feature map is:

  • A. \(6\times6\)
  • B. \(3\times3\)
  • C. \(5\times5\)
  • D. \(4\times4\)
Answer D. \(6-3+1=4\) positions in each direction.

Q5. Using \(n_{\text{out}}=\lfloor(n+2p-k)/s\rfloor+1\), a \(128\times128\) input with a \(5\times5\) kernel, \(p=2\), \(s=2\) gives:

  • A. \(62\times62\)
  • B. \(63\times63\)
  • C. \(64\times64\)
  • D. \(128\times128\)
Answer C. \(\lfloor127/2\rfloor+1=63+1=64\).

Q6. How many parameters has a layer of 64 filters of size \(3\times3\) applied to a 32-channel input?

  • A. 18,496
  • B. 18,432
  • C. 576
  • D. 2,112
Answer A. \((3\cdot3\cdot32+1)\times64=289\times64=18{,}496\).

Q7. A \(2\times2\) max-pooling layer with stride 2 has how many learned parameters?

  • A. 4
  • B. 1 per channel
  • C. 0
  • D. Equal to the number of input channels squared
Answer C. Pooling is a fixed operation; it only downsamples.

Q8. The \(2\times2\) max pool of the block \(\begin{pmatrix}9&9\\18&18\end{pmatrix}\) is:

  • A. 13.5
  • B. 54
  • C. 9
  • D. 18
Answer D. Max pooling keeps the largest value; average pooling would give 13.5.

Q9. What does ReLU do to a feature map from a right-edge detector applied to an image that has only a left edge?

  • A. Doubles every value
  • B. Sets the whole map to zero
  • C. Leaves it unchanged
  • D. Flips every sign
Answer B. All responses are negative, and \(\max(0,z)\) removes them: the map says there is no right edge.

Q10. In the lecture's 10-class SAR CNN (three conv blocks, GAP, dense), the total parameter count is:

  • A. 93,962
  • B. 524,416
  • C. 92,672
  • D. 1,290
Answer A. \(320+18{,}496+73{,}856+1{,}290=93{,}962\), about one-fifth of a single dense layer on the flattened chip.

Q11. After two \(3\times3\) convolutions with stride 1, one unit's receptive field is:

  • A. \(3\times3\)
  • B. \(6\times6\)
  • C. \(9\times9\)
  • D. \(5\times5\)
Answer D. \(r=1+2+2=5\). Stacking small kernels grows the view while keeping parameters low.

Q12. Because one kernel is used at every position, its gradient during backpropagation is:

  • A. Taken only from the first position
  • B. The average of the input pixels
  • C. The sum of the contributions from all positions
  • D. Zero
Answer C. \(\partial L/\partial K_{m,n}=\sum_{i,j}\delta_{i,j}x_{i+m,j+n}\).

Q13. Residual connections \(y=F(x)+x\) made very deep CNNs trainable mainly because:

  • A. They remove the need for ReLU
  • B. \(\partial y/\partial x=\partial F/\partial x+I\) gives the gradient a direct path
  • C. They halve the number of parameters
  • D. They replace pooling
Answer B. The identity term keeps gradients from vanishing however small \(\partial F/\partial x\) becomes.

Q14. A SAR recogniser reports 97% accuracy on chips collected at the same depression angle as its training data. The most important next question is:

  • A. Which optimiser was used?
  • B. How many GPUs trained it?
  • C. What is the accuracy under new angles, configurations and camouflage?
  • D. What colour map displays the chips?
Answer C. Standard-condition accuracy can collapse under distribution shift; operating conditions decide fielded performance.

Q15. The fast gradient sign method creates an adversarial example by:

  • A. Adding \(\varepsilon\,\text{sign}(\nabla_xL)\) to the input
  • B. Retraining the network on noisy data
  • C. Rotating the image by 90 degrees
  • D. Lowering the learning rate
Answer A. Backpropagating to the input finds the direction that most increases the loss; a tiny step can flip the class.

Part B — Assignment

Instructions. Five problems, 100 marks, plus a 20-mark bonus problem (Problem 6). Reference: Lecture 7 reference text.

Problem 1: Convolution, ReLU and pooling by hand 20 marks

A $6\times6$ image is 0 everywhere except a bright vertical bar of value 6 in rows 1–4, columns 2–3 (rows and columns numbered from 0). Use $K_1=\begin{pmatrix}-1&0&1\\-1&0&1\\-1&0&1\end{pmatrix}$, stride 1, no padding, bias 0.

(a) Compute the outputs at positions $(0,0)$, $(1,0)$ and $(0,2)$. [6]
(b) Write the full $4\times4$ feature map. [5]
(c) Apply ReLU, then $2\times2$ max pooling with stride 2. [5]
(d) Repeat (c) for $-K_1$ and interpret both pooled maps. [4]

Show full solution

(a) $(0,0)$: rows 0–2, columns 0–2; only column 2 (weight $+1$) holds bright pixels, in rows 1 and 2: $6+6=\mathbf{12}$. $(1,0)$: rows 1–3, column 2 bright in all three rows: $\mathbf{18}$. $(0,2)$: columns 2–4; column 2 ($-1$) is bright in rows 1–2, column 4 ($+1$) is dark: $\mathbf{-12}$.

(b) $\begin{pmatrix}12&12&-12&-12\\18&18&-18&-18\\18&18&-18&-18\\12&12&-12&-12\end{pmatrix}$. Positive on the bar's left edge (dark to bright), negative on its right edge (bright to dark).

(c) ReLU keeps the left half and zeros the right: $\begin{pmatrix}12&12&0&0\\18&18&0&0\\18&18&0&0\\12&12&0&0\end{pmatrix}$. Max pool: $\begin{pmatrix}18&0\\18&0\end{pmatrix}$.

(d) $-K_1$ flips every sign; after ReLU and pooling: $\begin{pmatrix}0&18\\0&18\end{pmatrix}$. Together the two maps say “a rising edge on the left, a falling edge on the right, along the full height”: a vertical bar. A deeper layer can combine these two channels into a “bar” detector.

Problem 2: The output-size formula 20 marks

Use $n_{\text{out}}=\lfloor(n+2p-k)/s\rfloor+1$.

(a) A $224\times224$ input meets a $7\times7$ convolution with $p=3$, $s=2$, followed by $3\times3$ max pooling with $p=1$, $s=2$. Give both output sizes. [6]
(b) A $64\times64$ input meets a $5\times5$ convolution with no padding and stride 1. [3]
(c) What padding keeps the size unchanged (“same”) for a $k\times k$ kernel with stride 1? Check it for $k=3$ and $k=5$. [5]
(d) A $32\times32$ map meets a $3\times3$ convolution with $p=1$, $s=2$. Compare this downsampling with $2\times2$ max pooling. [6]

Show full solution

(a) Convolution: $\lfloor(224+6-7)/2\rfloor+1=\lfloor111.5\rfloor+1=\mathbf{112}$. Pooling: $\lfloor(112+2-3)/2\rfloor+1=55+1=\mathbf{56}$. (This is the stem of ResNet.)

(b) $64-5+1=\mathbf{60}$: each layer without padding trims $k-1$ pixels.

(c) $n+2p-k+1=n\Rightarrow p=(k-1)/2$: $p=1$ for $k=3$, $p=2$ for $k=5$. Check: $(n+2-3)+1=n$ and $(n+4-5)+1=n$.

(d) $\lfloor(32+2-3)/2\rfloor+1=15+1=\mathbf{16}$, the same as $2\times2$ max pooling. The strided convolution downsamples with learned weights and $(9C_{\text{in}}+1)C_{\text{out}}$ parameters; pooling is fixed, parameter-free and gives some tolerance to small shifts.

Problem 3: Parameter counts for an EO recogniser 20 marks

A recogniser for $128\times128$ RGB chips has: Conv A 32 filters $3\times3$; Conv B 64 filters $3\times3$; Conv C 128 filters $3\times3$; Conv D 64 filters $1\times1$; global average pooling; dense 6-class output. Each conv uses “same” padding and is followed by $2\times2$ max pooling, except Conv D.

(a) Count the parameters of each layer and the total. [8]
(b) Give the output shape after each layer. [6]
(c) How many parameters would a single dense layer from the flattened input to 64 units need? Comment. [4]
(d) What does the $1\times1$ convolution do? [2]

Show full solution

(a) Conv A $(27+1)\times32=896$; Conv B $(288+1)\times64=18{,}496$; Conv C $(576+1)\times128=73{,}856$; Conv D $(128+1)\times64=8{,}256$; dense $64\times6+6=390$. Total $\mathbf{101{,}894}$.

(b) $128^2\times32\to$ pool $64^2\times32$; $64^2\times64\to32^2\times64$; $32^2\times128\to16^2\times128$; Conv D $16^2\times64$; GAP $64$; dense $6$.

(c) $128\times128\times3\times64+64=\mathbf{3{,}145{,}792}$: about 31 times the whole CNN, for one layer that ignores locality and position tolerance.

(d) It mixes channels at each pixel without looking at neighbours, here halving 128 channels to 64 cheaply before pooling (as in GoogLeNet's Inception modules).

Problem 4: Receptive fields 20 marks

Use $r_\ell=r_{\ell-1}+(k_\ell-1)j_{\ell-1}$ and $j_\ell=j_{\ell-1}s_\ell$, starting from $r_0=j_0=1$.

(a) Compute $r$ and $j$ after each layer of: conv $3\times3$ s1, conv $3\times3$ s1, pool $2\times2$ s2, conv $3\times3$ s1, pool $2\times2$ s2, conv $3\times3$ s1. [8]
(b) Do the same for the lecture's SAR CNN (conv, pool, conv, pool, conv, pool). Does a unit after Pool3 “see” the whole $64\times64$ chip? How does the classifier still use the whole chip? [8]
(c) Why do designers prefer two $3\times3$ convolutions to one $5\times5$? [4]

Show full solution

(a) conv: $r=3,j=1$; conv: $r=5,j=1$; pool: $r=5+1\times1=6,j=2$; conv: $r=6+2\times2=10,j=2$; pool: $r=10+1\times2=12,j=4$; conv: $r=12+2\times4=\mathbf{20}$, $j=4$.

(b) Conv1 $r=3$; Pool1 $r=4,j=2$; Conv2 $r=8$; Pool2 $r=10,j=4$; Conv3 $r=18$; Pool3 $r=\mathbf{22},j=8$. A single unit sees only $22\times22$ pixels, not the whole chip. Global average pooling then averages the $8\times8$ grid of such units, which together tile the chip, so the classifier draws on every region.

(c) Same $5\times5$ receptive field, fewer parameters ($2\times9C^2=18C^2$ against $25C^2$) and an extra ReLU, hence more non-linearity: the VGG design principle.

Problem 5: Evaluation brief for a SAR recogniser 20 marks

A vendor's CNN reports 97% accuracy on SAR chips collected at the same depression angle as its training data. Your test at a new angle gives correct counts T-72 180/200, BMP-2 150/200, ZSU-23-4 120/200.

(a) Compute per-class recall and overall accuracy at the new angle. [4]
(b) In an area with 20 T-72s and 1,000 other vehicles, the system flags T-72 with recall 0.90 and false-positive rate 2% on other vehicles. Compute true flags, false flags and precision. [6]
(c) Write a five-point brief for the commander on what must be checked before fielding. [10]

Show full solution

(a) Recall 90%, 75%, 60%; overall $450/600=\mathbf{75\%}$, against the reported 97%. The air-defence vehicle, perhaps the most important, degrades most.

(b) True flags $0.9\times20=18$; false flags $0.02\times1{,}000=20$; precision $18/38=\mathbf{47\%}$. More than half the T-72 calls are wrong, because targets are rare (the base-rate effect of Lecture 1).

(c) (1) Operating conditions: test on new angles, vehicle configurations, camouflage, terrain and decoys; report per-class recall, not one accuracy figure. (2) Base rate: state expected precision at realistic target densities. (3) Calibration: check that a 0.9 softmax means right about 90% of the time; flag low-confidence calls. (4) Shortcuts: Grad-CAM saliency should highlight the vehicle, not background or sensor artefacts. (5) Adversarial robustness and oversight: test against physical patches and spoofing; keep an analyst confirming every high-consequence call.

Problem 6 (bonus): Gradients through max pooling and a shared kernel 20 marks

A 1-D layer: input $x=(2,-1,3,0,1)$, kernel $(k_0,k_1)=(1,2)$, $y_i=k_0x_i+k_1x_{i+1}$, then ReLU, then max pooling of size 2, stride 2 on the first four outputs. The errors arriving at the two pooled outputs are $(1,-2)$.

(a) Compute $y$, the ReLU output and the pooled output. [4]
(b) Route the errors back through pooling and ReLU to get $\delta_i=\partial L/\partial y_i$. [5]
(c) Compute $\partial L/\partial k_0$, $\partial L/\partial k_1$ and $\partial L/\partial b$. [6]
(d) Compute $\partial L/\partial x_i$ for all five inputs, and the updated kernel with $\eta=0.1$. [5]

Show full solution

(a) $y=(2-2,\ -1+6,\ 3+0,\ 0+2)=(0,5,3,2)$; ReLU leaves it $(0,5,3,2)$; pooling over $(0,5)$ and $(3,2)$ gives $(5,3)$.

(b) Max pooling sends each error only to the winner: $\delta=(0,1,-2,0)$. ReLU passes it where $y>0$ ($y_1=5$, $y_2=3$), so $\delta=(0,1,-2,0)$.

(c) Weight sharing sums over positions: $\partial L/\partial k_0=\sum\delta_ix_i=1(-1)+(-2)(3)=\mathbf{-7}$; $\partial L/\partial k_1=\sum\delta_ix_{i+1}=1(3)+(-2)(0)=\mathbf{3}$; $\partial L/\partial b=\sum\delta_i=\mathbf{-1}$.

(d) $x_0$: $\delta_0k_0=0$. $x_1$: $\delta_0k_1+\delta_1k_0=1$. $x_2$: $\delta_1k_1+\delta_2k_0=2-2=0$. $x_3$: $\delta_2k_1+\delta_3k_0=-4$. $x_4$: $\delta_3k_1=0$. So $\partial L/\partial x=(0,1,0,-4,0)$. Update: $k\leftarrow(1+0.7,\ 2-0.3)=(\mathbf{1.7},\ \mathbf{1.7})$.
